When a free radical meets a molecule, where does it attack? For benzene the
answer is obvious, because every carbon is the same. For azulene, pyrene or a
new ligand nobody has made before, it isn't, and finding out in the lab
takes time, material and sometimes a particle accelerator.

Radmap is a program I wrote as a chemistry student at Saint Mary's
University in Halifax, in the Atlantic Centre for Green Chemistry, to answer
that question computationally. It shows the earliest moment of a reaction
between a molecule and a single atom (a hydrogen atom, a proton, a hydride, a
bromine atom or anything else monoatomic) as a colored surface wrapped
around the molecule. Red marks the low-energy spots, where the incoming atom
is drawn in and a radical is most likely to add.

We described the method in the *Journal of Molecular Graphics and
Modelling*:

> Andrew K. Long, Jason A. Fawcett, Jason A.C. Clyburne and Cory C. Pye.
> [RADMAP: Simple probes for rapid assessment of complex reactivity: A method
> and case studies on the reaction of hydrogen atoms with unsaturated organic
> molecules](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmgm.2016.01.002). *J. Mol. Graph.
> Model.* **64**, 147–152 (2016).

## Why muons

The problem came from muon chemistry. A positive muon that captures an
electron forms muonium, which behaves chemically like a hydrogen atom with
about a ninth of the mass. Fire muons into a sample and the muonium adds to
the molecules in it, just as a hydrogen atom would. In a transverse-field muon
spin resonance (µSR) experiment, each distinct radical that forms gives its
own pair of signals.

So before running an experiment you want to know how many places the muonium
can add, and roughly how much of each adduct will form. That tells you how
many signals to look for and how strong each should be. Beam time at a muon
facility is scarce, and a good prediction makes it go further.

## How it works

![Four panels explaining Radmap. Top left: the question, azulene plus a hydrogen atom gives what? Top right: a 3D model of azulene with a hydrogen atom placed above it and arrows showing the atom being moved around the molecule. Bottom left: azulene inside an orange wireframe surface. Bottom right: the same surface filled in and colored from blue to red.]({{ "/images/radmap/radmap-scan.jpg" | absolute_url }})
*Azulene + H·: the question, a probe atom moved over the molecule, the
surface it samples, and the finished map.*

Radmap works in four steps:

1. **Build a surface.** Starting from a structure loaded from a `.mol` file,
   Radmap draws a solvent-accessible surface around the molecule. Every point
   on it sits a fixed distance outside the atoms (by default a 1.2 Å probe
   radius beyond each atom's van der Waals radius), which is what
   *isodistance* means here. A resolution setting controls how many points
   the mesh has.
2. **Place the probe.** For every point on the mesh, Radmap writes a Gaussian
   input file containing the molecule plus the probe atom at that point, and
   a batch file to run them all. You choose the probe, the charge and
   multiplicity, and the method and basis set. The default is a short
   two-step optimization at the unrestricted B3LYP/6-31G level, which suits
   an open-shell radical.
3. **Compute.** Gaussian runs every job. This is the slow part: one quantum
   chemistry calculation per point on the surface, which is why the batch
   file can be split across machines.
4. **Paint.** Radmap reads the energy out of each output file, smooths the
   values onto the displayed surface with a Gaussian-weighted average, and
   colors it from blue (high energy, where the probe is pushed away) to red
   (low energy, where the probe is drawn in).

The result is a potential energy surface that isn't a flat slice through
space but wraps the whole molecule, so every side of it can be compared
at once. The red regions predict where addition happens. The relative
energies of the red spots also give a qualitative estimate of how much of
each adduct forms, which says something about the molecule's kinetic
stability as well as its reactivity.

The interface is built on [Jmol](https://jmol.sourceforge.net/), the open
source molecular viewer, so the finished map can be rotated and inspected
like any other Jmol model. Radmap itself is open source too.

## Reading the maps

![Radmap surface around pyridine, mostly green and blue, with a strong red patch just outside the nitrogen atom and blue over the center of the ring.]({{ "/images/radmap/radmap-pyridine-hydrogen-radical.jpg" | absolute_url }})
*Pyridine + H·. The strongest red sits just outside the nitrogen, and the
center of the ring is blue.*

Pyridine is a good first example because it has only one feature that
isn't a C–H. The map picks it out straight away: the hot spot is at the
nitrogen, and the ring carbons are all much cooler.

![Radmap surface around pyrene, with red patches over the outer carbon atoms, strongest on the upper and lower double bonds and at the carbons nearest the ring junctions, and blue over the center of each ring.]({{ "/images/radmap/radmap-pyrene-hdot-white.jpg" | absolute_url }})
*Pyrene + H·. Red over the outer carbons, weaker at the 2 and 7 positions,
and blue over the ring centers.*

Pyrene is where the method starts to earn its keep. It has 10 C–H carbons,
and the map separates them into groups: the carbons at the ends of the
upper and lower double bonds and the carbons next to the ring junctions
glow red, while the carbons in the middle of the left and right edges are
noticeably cooler. Each distinct red site is a distinct radical, and a
distinct µSR signal.

![Radmap surface around carbon disulfide, a linear S=C=S molecule. The surface is red at both sulfur ends, has blue bands over the carbon–sulfur bonds, and is green in the middle over the carbon.]({{ "/images/radmap/radmap-cs2-opt.jpg" | absolute_url }})
*Carbon disulfide + H·. The low-energy regions are the caps over the two
sulfur atoms, not the carbon.*

![Radmap surface around 1,3-dimethylimidazol-2-ylidene, a five-membered ring with two N-methyl groups. A red patch sits over the carbene carbon between the two nitrogens, and the rest of the surface is green and blue.]({{ "/images/radmap/radmap-dimethylimidazole.jpg" | absolute_url }})
*1,3-Dimethylimidazol-2-ylidene, an N-heterocyclic carbene, + H·. The red
patch is over the carbene carbon between the two nitrogens.*

The carbene is a nice check. Earlier muonium experiments from the Clyburne
group, with Paul Percival's team, [showed that the hydrogen atom adds to the
carbene carbon](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja028770t), and that is exactly where
Radmap puts the red.

![Radmap surface around a phosphorus pincer compound. A single strong red patch sits over the central phosphorus atom, and the rest of the surface is green and blue.]({{ "/images/radmap/radmap-p-pincer-low-rez.jpg" | absolute_url }})
*A phosphorus pincer compound + H·. The reactive site is the phosphorus at
the center.*

This is also where Radmap is useful beyond muons. When you design a new
ligand to bind to a metal, you want to know where its reactive sites are
before you spend weeks making it. A Radmap surface, run with whichever probe
fits the question, gives a quick first look.

![Radmap surface around azulene, rendered in grayscale, showing the smooth mesh that wraps the five- and seven-membered rings.]({{ "/images/radmap/radmap-azulene-BW.jpg" | absolute_url }})
*Azulene, rendered in grayscale.*

## Limitations

Radmap is deliberately simple, and that has costs:

- **It only sees the first contact.** The probe is placed at a fixed
  distance and relaxed for only a couple of steps, so the map shows where an
  atom is drawn in, not the final product or the barrier to get there.
- **It's expensive.** Every point on the mesh is a separate quantum chemistry
  job. A fine mesh on a large molecule means thousands of calculations.
- **It's qualitative.** The colors rank sites against each other on one
  molecule. They aren't rate constants, and the level of theory limits how
  far the numbers can be trusted.

Within those limits, it turns a question that used to need an experiment
into a picture you can rotate on screen.
